Lesson 01 · The rise of NPS

How one number conquered the boardroom

In 2003, Fred Reichheld brought NPS to a broad management audience through his Harvard Business Review article, “The One Number You Need to Grow”. The promise was compelling: rather than drowning in customer-satisfaction metrics, organizations could rally around one question and one memorable score.

  • A remarkably simple question. Ask customers how likely they are to recommend a company to a friend or colleague, on a 0–10 scale.
  • A number executives could understand. Subtract the percentage of detractors (0–6) from promoters (9–10). The result ranges from −100 to +100 points.
  • A common language for the business. A single metric can travel across leadership meetings, departments, markets and reporting periods.
  • A compelling promise. NPS was promoted not just as a measure of customer sentiment but as an indicator linked to loyalty and growth.
  • A challenge to its bigger claims. Later research questioned whether NPS predicts business performance better than alternative customer measures, and what information gets lost along the way.

The genius of NPS is its simplicity: one number can help focus an organization on its customers. But a useful management KPI is not necessarily a complete research measure. The calculation that makes NPS easy to communicate also discards information about the original responses.

Lesson 02 · What the number hides

Same NPS. Different realities.

Imagine two customer groups reporting exactly the same NPS. On a leadership dashboard, they look identical—same KPI, perhaps even the same target achievement. But beneath the headline, their responses can tell very different stories.

  • Eleven options become three categories. Scores of 0 and 6 both count as detractors, despite representing very different ratings.
  • Identical NPS does not imply identical customers. One group can be fairly uniform while another is sharply polarized.
  • Passives are still part of the story. Although they do not contribute directly to the NPS numerator, they can represent most of a customer group.
  • Business decisions can differ. Two products, markets or segments with the same score may deserve different follow-up investigations.
  • The original 0–10 distribution matters. The headline number cannot replace a view of the response shape.

Key takeaways from Lesson 2

At NPS 0, a group in which everyone responds 7 looks identical on the headline KPI to a group split evenly between 0 and 10. Yet the former is homogeneous and has a mean rating of 7.0, while the latter is polarized and averages 5.0.

Neither NPS nor the mean alone tells the whole story. Report the original 0–10 distribution and the promoter, passive and detractor shares alongside the KPI—and investigate the reasons rather than inferring them from the shape.

Research behind this lesson: Reichheld (2003); Kristensen & Eskildsen (2014).

Lesson 03 · Measurement context

Same bank. Different moments.

Imagine you lead customer experience at a digital bank. Customers who have just set up a recurring investment report NPS +75; customers who have contacted support report −40. Does that prove investment is excellent and support is failing? Not necessarily. First, we need to know what was measured, when, and among whom.

  • The moment changes the question. Evaluating an interaction immediately afterwards is different from judging your overall relationship with the bank.
  • Transactional and relationship NPS are not interchangeable. A support-conversation survey and a bank-wide recommendation survey capture different constructs.
  • Who is surveyed matters. People contacting support may differ systematically from those setting up investments.
  • Wording and timing matter too. A question asked immediately after an interaction may elicit a different judgment than one asked weeks later.
  • The survey mix can move the headline. Pooled NPS can change even when no touchpoint-level score changes.

Key takeaways from Lesson 3

An NPS result needs its measurement context. In this fictional example, equal-weight touchpoint surveys produce a pooled NPS of about +29. When investment surveys become more common, it rises to +53—without any touchpoint score improving.

This is a composition effect: the aggregate changes because the relative weights of its component groups change. A mathematically correct pooled score can still answer the wrong business question, particularly when the underlying surveys evaluate different experiences.

Before comparing NPS across markets, products, segments or time, check the surveyed populations, triggers, wording and collection windows. Interpret changes within comparable groups rather than relying on the pooled number alone.

Research behind this lesson: Kristensen & Eskildsen (2014) (broader measurement critique). The composition effect here is a mathematical demonstration, not an empirical finding about banks.

Lesson 04 · Tracking & uncertainty

Your NPS increased by six points. Should you celebrate?

September’s NPS was +30. October’s is +36. The dashboard shows a green arrow; perhaps a product improvement gets the credit. But how much of that six-point movement could simply reflect which customers happened to respond?

  • NPS is usually an estimate, not a census. Different samples can yield different scores even if the underlying customer population is unchanged.
  • Sample size affects precision. Small samples tend to fluctuate more, so a dashboard change may reflect sampling noise.
  • No universal minimum n works for every NPS survey. Precision depends on sample size and the promoter/passive/detractor distribution.
  • Equal NPS can mean unequal uncertainty. Polarized and largely passive populations can need very different samples to achieve the same margin of error.
  • Comparing waves is a separate statistical task. We must account for uncertainty in both months, not just draw a confidence interval around one score.

Methodology appendix · Sampling assumptions and formula

Each simulated respondent contributes X = +1, 0 or −1, with fixed probabilities. The NPS estimator is 100 × the sample mean of X. Its theoretical variance is Var(X) = p(promoter) + p(detractor) − [p(promoter) − p(detractor)]².

Approximate required n = ceiling[1.96² × 10,000 × Var(X) / desired_margin²]. Population A variance = 0.21; Population B variance = 0.91. The planning calculation assumes a 95% normal approximation and known theoretical population proportions.

Key takeaways from Lesson 4

Reported NPS is an estimate with uncertainty. At a 95% confidence level, achieving an approximate ±10-point margin of error requires about 81 responses for Population A but 350 for Population B, despite both having true NPS +30. Their variances differ.

Larger samples improve sampling precision but do not automatically resolve nonresponse bias or inconsistent recruitment. For tracked NPS, report uncertainty and assess changes with a comparison suited to the two survey waves—rather than reading statistical significance into a dashboard arrow.

Lesson 05 · Beyond the score

Customers gave us a number. Now let’s listen.

Imagine our bank has built a rigorous NPS programme. We know how customers responded, have comparable measures, and report uncertainty. But even a perfectly measured NPS does not tell us what to improve. For that, we need to understand what customers say about their experiences.

  • A score tells us how customers rated—not why. Fees, reliability, support, usability and unmet expectations can all sit behind the same number.
  • Open-ended feedback can surface unanticipated issues. Customers may raise experiences we never thought to include in a predefined survey list.
  • Question wording shapes the responses. Asking only detractors what went wrong and only promoters what they liked makes the resulting themes harder to compare.
  • Text can be connected to quantitative data. Coded topics let us examine what people mention and how those mentions relate to their original 0–10 rating.
  • Association is not causation. A topic linked to a lower rating does not tell us that changing it will increase NPS.

This lesson has two parts: first, design a comparable open-ended question; then explore a fictional text-informed driver analysis.

Part A · Ask better questions, get comparable answers

If we ask detractors what went wrong, passives what could improve, and promoters what they liked, we are steering different score groups toward different kinds of answers. Differences in the themes we uncover may therefore reflect our question wording—not simply what customers chose to share. That makes cross-score comparisons and text-informed key-driver analysis much harder to interpret.

A better starting point: ask everyone the same neutral question: “Why did you give this score?”

What are customers talking about?

Methodology appendix · Text coding, modeling and limitations

The dataset contains 1,600 wholly synthetic banking-app respondents. Every respondent is asked exactly: “What is the main reason for the score you gave?” The generator produces one or two short fictional statements from a predefined phrase bank. Transparent rule-based topic indicators are stored as topic_* columns. A response can contain more than one topic. A topic is coded when mentioned regardless of whether the phrase praises or criticizes it.

The model is ordinary least squares: recommendation_score ~ seven topic indicators + customer segment + tenure. HC3 heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors produce the plotted intervals. The 0–10 score—not an invented individual NPS—is the outcome. The R script independently fits the same specification.

Crucially, the synthetic data were generated with deliberately designed experience–score relationships, including noise. The displayed coefficients are estimated from that dataset; they are not empirical banking findings or evidence of causal effects. Topic mentions are endogenous to respondents' chosen explanations, non-mentions do not mean an experience was absent, and short phrases do not capture full qualitative nuance. The model adjusts for listed predictors only. Correlated topics, omitted factors, imperfect coding, respondent selection and score-conditioned explanations can distort interpretation. The model's coefficient does not predict how much NPS would change after an intervention. Human review and validation of text coding are required in a real study.

Key takeaways from Lesson 5

Customer feedback is most useful when we connect what people say to how they rate—but never treat either as sufficient on its own. A common topic is not necessarily the most important business priority; a statistical association does not establish an effective intervention.

Use open-ended answers to discover experiences, quantitative analysis to identify patterns worth investigating, and further research or experimentation to test potential remedies. The result is not a guaranteed recipe for increasing NPS, but a more evidence-led starting point for action.

Putting it together

Five habits of a better NPS programme

You can keep the KPI and still be a more critical researcher. A practical programme pairs the simplicity of NPS with evidence that makes the number interpretable and actionable.

  1. Keep the headline, preserve the detail. Report the full 0–10 distribution and category shares, not just the final score.
  2. Compare like with like. Distinguish relationship from transactional measures; document survey triggers, timing, questions and population mix.
  3. Report uncertainty. Match sample planning to expected variance, and test changes across waves rather than celebrating arrows.
  4. Ask the same neutral “why” question. Invite comparable, open-ended explanations and review the resulting text for nuance.
  5. Turn associations into investigations. Combine topic frequency, modeled patterns and other evidence; validate proposed improvements before claiming causal impact.

NPS can start a conversation. Good research is what moves the conversation forward.
